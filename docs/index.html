Lost & Sound

An audio-only navigation game built with JavaScript, Web Audio and Resonance Audio. You wear headphones and close your eyes. 3D sound tells you where the ghost-story enemies are: they speed up and rise in pitch as they get closer. A layered “home” sound grows richer as you walk toward it.

▶ PLAY GAME Please use headphones
Demo video coming soon

Why I made it

When I used Logic Pro, I kept wondering what was behind it: how reverb creates space, how EQ changes frequencies. Everything in Logic starts from recorded sound. I wanted to try building sound from zero, in code.

I also asked a simple question: if there are no visuals, how much can people perceive by ear alone? So I made a game that is only about sound. Most games depend on what you see. An audio-only game could also be played by people who are blind or have low vision.

How the sound works

The listener

The player is a listener in a Resonance Audio scene. You walk and turn with the arrow keys, at 3 meters per second. Your position and direction are updated every frame, so sounds move around your head as you walk and turn.

The map

Every game is different. The four enemies are placed in random directions, in four rings around you: about 3.5–5 m, 6–7.5 m, 8–9.5 m and 10–11.5 m away. Home is 13 m away, also in a random direction. You win when you get within half a meter of home.

Enemies

Each of the four enemies is a looping sound I designed. When you are farther than 3 m, it plays normally. As you get closer, its playback rate goes up, so it gets faster and higher at the same time. At 0.5 m it plays at double speed, one octave higher.

Home

The home sound has a main loop, plus one pad sample played at three speeds: 0.5×, 1× and 2× (low, middle and high octave). It starts to change when you are 8 m away. As you get closer, the main loop fades out and the pad layers come in one by one: low first, then middle, then high. I chose the speeds and volume curves by testing them again and again by ear. This was the most surprising part for me.

Home sound: volume of each layer by distance

Closeness goes from 0 (8 m or farther) to 1 (within 0.5 m of home). The main loop fades out while the low, middle and high pad layers come in, in that order.

Hearing front and back

My teacher, working with an AI assistant, wrote an air-absorption filter and a filter to reduce front/back confusion. I added them to the game and tuned the front/back values by ear.

The space

The game takes place in a large open space, 100 × 100 m and 3 m high. The walls are open and the floor and ceiling are grass, so there are almost no hard echoes.

To set this up, we put a small file called .role in the project folder. It is not uploaded to GitHub, so each of us has our own copy. On Marc’s computer it says “teacher”, so the AI helps him build the framework and plan each step. On my computer it says “student”, so the AI does not write the code for me. It explains one idea at a time and helps me work through the plan step by step. This way I wrote my parts myself and can explain every line.

SCAMP Piano Pieces

Three short piano pieces I wrote with Python and the SCAMP library: “Clown”, “Q and A” and “Bellicose Bunnies”.

Performed by me.

Process

I created the music in three steps. The first two steps were done through code, using SCAMP (Suite for Computer-Assisted Music in Python). SCAMP is a Python library that lets composers create virtual instruments, have them play notes, listen to the results in real time, and then generate music notation.

I defined functions to play different musical gestures, and the function arguments let me vary the gestures in different ways so that they could evolve throughout the piece. During this step, I was not thinking about how the music would be notated. I was thinking like a pianist about how each phrase breathes and moves.

The second step was to generate notation. To do this, I had to tweak the code: making the note durations easier to notate, and defining meters and bar lengths. After that, SCAMP could produce MusicXML that I opened in MuseScore.

The final step was polishing that raw notation into the final score. This was not just cleaning up rough edges; it was another stage of composition. I added more layers to the music, along with dynamics, articulations and other details that were crucial to its character.

Coding gave me a different way to organize music. I took on different roles throughout the process — engineer, pianist, composer — and I arrived at music that I would never have otherwise written.

from scamp import *


s = Session()

s.tempo = 150

piano = s.new_part("piano")


def drop_down(start_pitch, repeats=1, rise=1):
    for x in range(repeats):
        piano.play_note(start_pitch + rise*x, 1.0, 2 / 3)
        piano.play_note(start_pitch + 1 + rise*x, 1.0, 1 / 6)
        piano.play_note(start_pitch + 3 + rise*x, 1.0, 1 / 6)

    piano.play_note(start_pitch - 2, 1.0, 1.0)


def jump(main_pitch, gesture_dur, quick_note_dur, last_note_dur, repeats=1, rise=1):
    long_note_dur = gesture_dur - quick_note_dur - last_note_dur
    for x in range(repeats):
        piano.play_note(main_pitch + 24 + rise*x, 1.0, quick_note_dur)
        piano.play_note(main_pitch, 1.0, long_note_dur)
        piano.play_note(main_pitch + 6 + rise*x, 1.0, last_note_dur, "staccato")


def tremolo(main_pitch, note_dur=0.25, repeats=8):
    for i in range(repeats):
        piano.play_note(main_pitch, 1.0, note_dur)
        piano.play_note(main_pitch + i + 1, 1.0, note_dur)


def the_clown():
    tremolo(48, note_dur=0.25, repeats=4)
    jump(44, 4, 0.25, 1, repeats=3, rise=2)
    wait(1)

    drop_down(60, repeats=4, rise=2)
    jump(60, 2, 0.125, 0.25, repeats=8, rise=1)
    wait(1)

    tremolo(80, note_dur=1/6, repeats=12)
    drop_down(84, repeats=3, rise=-3)
    drop_down(81, repeats=4, rise=-3)
    jump(40, 5, 0.5, 1, repeats=3, rise=-2)
    wait(1)

    tremolo(58, note_dur=0.25, repeats=5)
    piano.play_note(36, 1.0, 0.5, "accent")


s.start_transcribing()
s.fast_forward()
the_clown()
perf = s.stop_transcribing()
time_signatures = ["2/4", "4/4", "4/4", "5/4", "5/4"] + ["2/4"] * 7 + ["3/4", "4/4", "4/4", "5/4"] + ["5/4"] * 3 + ["7/8"] + ["2/4"]
score = perf.to_score(time_signature=time_signatures)
score.show()
print(perf)

def irritation_gesture(start_pitch, interval=1, wait_dur=2.0, repeats=3):
    for _ in range(repeats):
        fork(tremolo, args=(start_pitch, 0.025, 2))
        wait(wait_dur)
    fork(tremolo, args=(start_pitch + interval, 0.025, 2))
    wait(wait_dur)

Flowing Light

An audiovisual piece about the Yangtze River. I composed the music and made the video myself. The images in the video were generated by AI from my prompts.

Concept

Flowing Light combines the pentatonic scale, minimalist music and the rough shape of the Yangtze River's flow. With the Yangtze as its subject, it shows repetition and change inside a flow.

The shape of the river always seems familiar, but the light and color on the water never really stay still. This is what I wanted to express in this piece: simple musical fragments come back again and again, and you slowly start to notice the small changes in them.

In the video, the river runs through layered curves. It moves from black and white into color, and then its outline appears against a darkening background. You can still recognize the river's shape, but the colors around it keep changing. The same river shows many different faces.

I hope the audience can listen and watch the way you would watch water from the riverbank, slowly noticing the changes that are easy to miss: a familiar sound comes back, a color fades, and the river keeps moving forward.

Process

I composed the music mostly in a traditional way. I started from the pentatonic scale, wrote the first musical material, and let it develop and change as it came back. But this time I also tried a method I had never used before. Rhythmic drive is especially important in this piece, so after I finished the opening material, I first wrote out the rhythm of the whole piece from beginning to end. Then I divided this rhythm between the left and right hands of the piano, and built the melody and the other layers from there.

I made the video myself. The images in it were generated by AI from my prompts.
